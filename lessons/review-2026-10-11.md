> [!abstract] PI

Your half-life derivation was clean, and that was the piece that mattered. The one gap is *why* the mean is $1/k$, so here is the worked example.

**Mean exchange time, worked for $k = 0.5$ per second.**

The density $f(t) = k e^{-kt}$ is the fraction of sites exchanging per second at time $t$. The average exchange time is the usual weighted average, each $t$ weighted by the fraction exchanging there:

$$\langle t \rangle = \int_0^\infty t \cdot k e^{-kt}\, dt.$$

Integrate by parts with $u = t$ and $dv = k e^{-kt}\,dt$, so $du = dt$ and $v = -e^{-kt}$:

$$\langle t \rangle = \Big[-t\, e^{-kt}\Big]_0^\infty + \int_0^\infty e^{-kt}\, dt.$$

The boundary term is $0$ at both ends: at $t = 0$ because of the factor $t$, and as $t \to \infty$ because $e^{-kt}$ dies faster than $t$ grows. What remains is the area under the survival curve $e^{-kt}$:

$$\int_0^\infty e^{-kt}\, dt = \Big[-\tfrac{1}{k} e^{-kt}\Big]_0^\infty = 0 - \left(-\tfrac{1}{k}\right) = \tfrac{1}{k}.$$

With $k = 0.5$ per second that is $2$ s.

Reading: the mean lifetime is literally "add up how long the population survives," the area under the survival fraction. The half-life you derived, $1.38$ s, is shorter than the mean $2$ s because the density is front-loaded and the long tail of late exchangers pulls the average up.

**D3. Where repeated steps go.** From $v_0 = (1000, 0)$ with $P = \begin{pmatrix} 0.8 & 0.2 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}$ the counts run

$$(1000, 0) \to (800, 200) \to (740, 260) \to (722, 278) \to (716.6, 283.4) \to \cdots \to (714.3, 285.7).$$

The steps shrink and the counts settle. Why? Each step, closed sites leaving is $0.2\,C$ and open sites returning is $0.5\,O$. At the start that is $200$ out and $0$ back. As $C$ falls and $O$ rises, the outflow shrinks and the return grows, and they must meet. The settled point is where they are equal:

$$0.2\,\pi_C = 0.5\,\pi_O \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\pi_C}{\pi_O} = \frac{0.5}{0.2} = 2.5 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \pi = \left(\tfrac{5}{7}, \tfrac{2}{7}\right) \approx (0.714, 0.286).$$

In matrix language that is $\pi P = \pi$: the distribution that the step rule maps to itself. "Stationary" describes the *counts*, not the sites. Individual sites keep flipping every step; the two flows just cancel. This is exactly the opening equilibrium in Linderstrøm-Lang: $\pi_O / \pi_C = K_{\text{op}}$, and you just saw that it is a ratio of the two switching probabilities.

**D4. From step matrices to a rate matrix.** $P$ depends on the step size, which is awkward: a different $dt$ gives a different $P$. Fix it with R1. Over a tiny slice $dt$, a closed site opens with probability $k_{\text{op}}\,dt$ and an open one closes with probability $k_{\text{cl}}\,dt$. So

$$P(dt) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - k_{\text{op}}\,dt & k_{\text{op}}\,dt \\ k_{\text{cl}}\,dt & 1 - k_{\text{cl}}\,dt \end{pmatrix} = I + Q\,dt, \qquad Q = \begin{pmatrix} -k_{\text{op}} & k_{\text{op}} \\ k_{\text{cl}} & -k_{\text{cl}} \end{pmatrix}.$$

$Q$ is the **generator** or rate matrix. Off the diagonal sit the rates of each jump. Each diagonal entry is minus the total rate of leaving that state, which makes every row sum to zero, the rate-matrix echo of "rows of $P$ sum to 1." $Q$ does not depend on $dt$; it is the thing that *is* the chain.

Now run the same move you made for one state. One slice: $v(t + dt) = v(t)(I + Q\,dt) = v(t) + v(t)\,Q\,dt$. Subtract, divide by $dt$:

$$\frac{dv}{dt} = v\,Q.$$

That is the **master equation**, and it is $dU/dt = -kU$ with a matrix in place of $k$. Check: one state, $Q = (-k)$, and it reads $dU/dt = -kU$. Stationary now means $\pi Q = 0$, which for two states is $\pi_C\,k_{\text{op}} = \pi_O\,k_{\text{cl}}$, the same balanced flows with rates instead of per-step probabilities.
